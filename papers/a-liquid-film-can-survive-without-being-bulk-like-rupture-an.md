arXiv:2611.25008v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: How thick must a liquid film be before its middle behaves like ordinary bulk liquid? Free-standing films are the standard geometry for computing surface properties by molecular simulation and the simplest model of soap and foam films, coatings and polymer layers, yet the question is seldom answered quantitatively. We answer it for Lennard-Jones films by comparing two criteria: whether the film survives without rupturing, and whether the lateral pressure at its center has relaxed to the bulk value. Covering three interaction ranges (cutoffs rc = 3.5-7.5 times sigma), three temperatures and lateral system sizes from 20 to 100 times sigma, we find that the two criteria give different thicknesses and respond to different parameters. Films thinner than 6-7 times sigma (about 2 nm for argon) rupture within 105 tau (~0.2 microseconds); this kinetic limit hardly depends on the interaction range but grows with the lateral size of the system, and close to it rupture is an activated event. The center becomes mechanically bulk-like only at a larger thickness h*, where the tangential anisotropy computed with the Irving-Kirkwood definition falls below 0.003 epsilon x sigma^3; h* grows from ~11 times sigma (3.8 nm) to ~15 times sigma (5 nm) as the cutoff increases from 3.5 to 7.5 times sigma, but it is nearly independent of temperature and of lateral size. Between the two limits films are long-lived yet perturbed throughout. Neither the surface tension, which shows no thickness dependence, nor the central density, whose relative deficit is about 16 times smaller than the relative anisotropy, reveals this perturbation: the stress detects confinement before the structure does.
