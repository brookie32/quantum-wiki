arXiv:2611.26889v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: The awkward disconnect in the postulates of quantum mechanics between the continuous, deterministic, linear, and unitary evolution governed by Schrodinger's equation and the discontinuous, stochastic, nonlinear, and nonunitary measurement process has vexed physicists for decades. Intuition from classical mechanics, statistical mechanics, and dynamical systems theory suggests that such a process might be modeled with a nonlinear system of differential equations. We explore such approaches by designing a deterministic model of an ideal projective measurement on a single qubit and constructing an analog computer that simulates it by manipulating voltages in an electrical circuit. The model reduces to Schrodinger evolution when no measurement is taking place. During a measurement, a hidden variable that the observer does not resolve selects the outcome, and the model is constructed so that a uniformly distributed hidden variable yields Born-rule frequencies. We do not derive the Born rule; rather, we show that a deterministic dynamical system with no intrinsic noise can implement it. Over 300 trials, the circuit reproduces the ideal model's individual outcomes to within one percent of the decision threshold, and its outcome frequencies match the Born rule to within about 0.02 in probability. These results are a small but provocative step towards modeling quantum measurement with macro-scale machines---a step of interest for both the foundations of physics and the alternatives to digital computing it suggests.
